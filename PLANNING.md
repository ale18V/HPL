### **Conflict of Interest**

**Question:** A major investor pressures you to skip a final round of safety testing to meet a market launch deadline. They argue that the robot is already "safe enough" and that any delay would risk the company's survival. Do you:  
**A:** proceed with the launch to satisfy investors and secure the company’s financial future  
**B:** insist on completing the safety testing, even if it risks losing investor support or delaying the project

**Effects:** A: -2 lives; B: +1 life, -1 coin

**Why it matters:** Financial pressure is a common source of ethical failure. Prioritizing investor satisfaction over verified safety can lead to catastrophic consequences for users.

**Real-life example:** This dilemma mirrors the internal pressure at Boeing regarding the 737 MAX. To compete with Airbus, Boeing rushed the development and pilot training for the 737 MAX. Critical safety systems like MCAS were downplayed to avoid expensive simulator training. The resulting crashes led to the loss of 346 lives and a permanent stain on Boeing's reputation.

**Reference:** *The New York Times*. "Boeing’s 737 Max: A Corporate Culture of Corner-Cutting." [https://www.nytimes.com/2019/11/17/business/boeing-737-max.html](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/11/17/business/boeing-737-max.html)

### **Supply Chain Ethics**

**Question:** Your robot’s high-performance batteries require cobalt and lithium. Your procurement team finds a supplier that is significantly cheaper but has a history of poor labor practices and child labor in its mining operations. Do you:  
**A:** use the cheaper supplier to keep the robot's final price accessible to more users  
**B:** source from an ethical supplier with third-party audits, even if it increases the robot's cost

**Effects:** A: -1 life; B: +1 life, -1 coin

**Why it matters:** Ethical engineering extends beyond the final product to the foundations of the supply chain. Using unethically sourced materials shifts the "cost" of the robot onto vulnerable children and workers.

**Real-life example:** Approximately 70% of the world's cobalt is mined in the Democratic Republic of Congo, where an estimated 40,000 children work in hazardous artisanal mines. Tech giants like Apple, Google, and Tesla have faced lawsuits over their alleged role in these supply chains.

**Reference:** *The Guardian*. "Apple and Google named in US lawsuit over Congolese child cobalt mining deaths." ### **Algorithmic Impact Assessment (AIA)**

**Question:** Before writing any code, you have the option to perform a thorough Algorithmic Impact Assessment (AIA) to identify potential biases and harms. This will delay the start of development by several weeks. Do you:  
**A:** perform the AIA to identify and mitigate risks early in the planning phase  
**B:** skip the AIA and start development immediately, relying on testing at the end of the project to catch issues

**Effects:** A: +1 life, -1 turn; B: -1 life

**Why it matters:** Risks are much easier and cheaper to fix when identified at the planning stage. Waiting until the end of development can lock in biased architectures that are difficult to change.

**Real-life example:** A widely used healthcare algorithm by Optum was found to favor white patients over Black patients because it used "healthcare cost" as a proxy for "health need." An AIA at the planning stage would have identified that cost is a biased metric, preventing a tool used on 200 million people from discriminating against millions of Black patients.

**Reference:** *Science*. "Dissecting racial bias in an algorithm used to manage the health of populations." [https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aax2342](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aax2342)

### **Bankruptcy & "Bricking"**

**Question:** Your robot relies on cloud servers for its AI processing. During the planning phase, you must decide whether to invest in an "offline mode" or an open-source backup plan in case the company goes bankrupt or shuts down its servers. Do you:  
**A:** invest in a local backup or open-source path to ensure the robot remains functional even if the company fails  
**B:** focus entirely on the cloud-based features to provide the best performance for current users

**Effects:** A: +1 life, -1 coin; B: -1 life

**Why it matters:** When cloud-dependent hardware is "bricked" by a company shutdown, users who rely on the device for their daily lives are abandoned, and the hardware becomes electronic waste.

**Real-life example:** When the social robot company Jibo shut down its servers in 2019, thousands of robots across the world became non-functional. The robots performed a final "goodbye dance" before losing their ability to interact, leaving users with expensive, useless hardware and a sense of grief.

**Reference:** *The Verge*. "Jibo is dying, and it’s a reminder of why we shouldn’t trust the cloud." [https://www.theverge.com/2019/3/4/18250004/jibo-social-robot-shutdown-servers-cloud-death](https://www.theverge.com/2019/3/4/18250004/jibo-social-robot-shutdown-servers-cloud-death)

### **Vulnerability Disclosure Policy**

**Question:** During the planning phase, you must decide how to handle potential security flaws reported by outsiders. Your development team is highly competent and follows industry-standard security protocols. Do you:  
**A:** rely on your internal team's expertise and standard proprietary audits to ensure the robot remains secure and cost-effective  
**B:** establish an explicit Bug Bounty program on your website that offers financial rewards for any discovered vulnerabilities

**Effects:** A: -1 life; B: +1 life, -1 coin

**Why it matters:** Even the most competent teams have blind spots. If you plan for a "closed" security model based on internal trust, you may miss critical flaws—like a bug that allows someone to remotely hijack the robot's video stream. Without an authorized disclosure path, you risk your robot being turned into a **hidden camera** inside users' homes before your team even realizes a flaw exists.

**Real-life example:** In 2016, **St. Jude Medical** chose to sue the researchers who discovered that their pacemakers could be remotely hacked, rather than rewarding them. The company insisted their internal testing was sufficient. The FDA eventually confirmed the vulnerabilities were real, proving that internal confidence is not a substitute for external auditing.

**Reference:** *Reuters*. "FDA confirms St. Jude pacemakers vulnerable to hacking." [https://www.reuters.com/article/technology/fda-confirms-st-jude-pacemakers-vulnerable-to-hacking-idUSKBN14T1X6/](https://www.reuters.com/article/technology/fda-confirms-st-jude-pacemakers-vulnerable-to-hacking-idUSKBN14T1X6/)

### **Budgeting for Accessibility**

**Question:** During the initial budget planning, your lead accountant suggests treating accessibility features (like screen readers, voice alternatives, and physical adjustments) as "optional extras" to be added after the core product is successful. Do you:  
**A:** earmark a significant portion of the initial budget for accessibility, treating it as a core requirement  
**B:** prioritize the "average user" experience first to ensure the product is commercially viable

**Effects:** A: +1 life, -1 coin; B: -1 life

**Why it matters:** Accessibility is much harder and more expensive to "bolt on" later. If it isn't part of the initial budget, it often gets cut when projects face the inevitable cost overruns.

**Real-life example:** Many early websites and mobile apps ignored accessibility standards, making them unusable for people with visual or motor impairments. This led to a wave of lawsuits under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), forcing companies to spend millions on retroactive fixes that could have been avoided with better planning.

**Reference:** *The New York Times*. "Websites are becoming more accessible, but for some, the progress is too slow." [https://www.nytimes.com/2020/02/20/business/accessibility-websites-lawsuits.html](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/02/20/business/accessibility-websites-lawsuits.html)

### **Regulatory Strategy**

**Question:** You can plan to launch the robot as a "wellness device" to avoid strict FDA regulations and get to market in 6 months. Or you can aim for "medical device" certification, which will take 2 years and require rigorous clinical evidence. Do you:  
**A:** commit to the longer medical certification path to ensure the highest safety and effectiveness standards  
**B:** launch as a wellness device first to start generating revenue and gather "real-world" data

**Effects:** A: +1 life, -2 turns; B: -1 life

**Why it matters:** Mislabeling a health-critical device as a wellness product can lead to users trusting it for medical tasks it hasn't been proven to handle safely.

**Real-life example:** In 2013, the FDA sent a warning letter to 23andMe, ordering them to stop selling their genetic health tests because they were being marketed for medical purposes (like predicting disease risk) without the necessary regulatory review to prove they were accurate and safe.

**Reference:** *U.S. Food and Drug Administration*. "Warning Letter to 23andMe, Inc." [https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/23andme-inc-22-nov-2013](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/23andme-inc-22-nov-2013)

### **Crisis Communication**

**Question:** During the planning phase, you are drafting the company's crisis response plan. Should the plan prioritize protecting the company's legal position or being fully transparent with the public and regulators if a robot causes an injury? Do you:  
**A:** prioritize full transparency and public safety in the crisis plan, even if it increases legal risk  
**B:** prioritize protecting the company’s reputation and legal standing in the event of an accident

**Effects:** A: +1 life, -1 turn; B: -1 life

**Why it matters:** When companies hide failures to protect their reputation, they prevent others from learning from the mistake and leave other users at risk of the same failure.

**Real-life example:** After the first fatal crash involving its Autopilot system, Tesla was criticized for not being transparent with the public about the limitations of the system and the details of the crash, leading to a long-running investigation by the NHTSA and a loss of public trust in autonomous technology.

**Reference:** *The Guardian*. "Tesla Autopilot crash: what we know and what it means for the future of self-driving cars." ### **Long-term Maintenance**

**Question:** You are planning the software maintenance lifecycle. Supporting older versions of the robot is expensive. Do you plan to provide software and security updates for 10 years, or "encourage" users to upgrade every 3 years by ending support for older models? Do you:  
**A:** commit to 10 years of software and security support to ensure the robot remains safe for long-term users  
**B:** plan for a 3-year support cycle to encourage hardware upgrades and keep development costs low

**Effects:** A: +1 life, -1 coin; B: -1 life

**Why it matters:** Assistive technology is expensive, and many users cannot afford to upgrade frequently. Ending support early makes the device unsafe to use and creates unnecessary electronic waste.

**Real-life example:** This dilemma is seen in the "end of life" support for smartphones and computers. Many perfectly functional devices become insecure or unusable because the manufacturer stops providing software updates, a practice often called "software-induced obsolescence."

### **Customer Support Strategy**

**Question:** You are planning the support system for your robot. A dedicated help desk staffed by experts trained in accessibility and crisis management is expensive to maintain. An automated AI chatbot is significantly cheaper and can handle 90% of technical queries. Do you:  
**A:** invest in a specialized 24/7 human support team to assist users with disabilities  
**B:** implement an AI-first support model to keep the robot's subscription price low

**Effects:** A: +1 life, -1 coin; B: -1 life

**Why it matters:** In assistive technology, a support request is often not just a "bug" but a barrier to basic living. If an automated system fails to understand a user with a speech impairment or an urgent safety need, the consequences are far more severe than a standard software glitch.

**Real-life example:** Apple is frequently cited as a leader in this area with its dedicated **Accessibility Support** line. Users can speak directly to specialists trained specifically in assistive features and diverse accessibility needs. This contrasts with many modern tech platforms that rely almost entirely on automated "self-service" bots, which can leave users with disabilities locked out or stranded when their unique needs aren't recognized by the algorithm.

**Reference:** *Apple Support*. "How to contact Apple for accessibility support." [https://support.apple.com/en-us/HT209585](https://support.apple.com/en-us/HT209585)
